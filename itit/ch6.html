Cloud Computing, IoT and Emerging Technologies

1. Cloud services

Cloud services

Shift computing activities from the user's desktop to computers on the internet. Cloud computing frees end users from owning, maintaining and storing software and data, and gives them access to those services from anywhere with an internet connection.

2. The three basic components

The essential elements of cloud computing are:

  1. Clients — the end users.
  2. Service providers — the companies operating the cloud.
  3. The internet — the medium connecting the two.

3. Two critical efficiency factors

Two factors determine how efficient cloud computing actually is:

  • The speed and reliability of the user's internet connection.
  • The internet's capability to provide safe and reliable transmission of the data.

4. Cloud deployment models

Deployment models answer who the cloud is for — not what it provides.

The four cloud deployment models
ModelWho it servesWho manages it
Public cloud The general public or large organisations. Owned and managed by the cloud provider.
Private cloud A single organisation, exclusively. Managed by the organisation itself or by a third party.
Hybrid cloud A mix of two or more types — both public and private. Split between the parties involved.
Community cloud Several organisations sharing a specific community, so they can share data and environments. Shared between the participating organisations.

The private model is the one preferred by organisations that need exclusive control over sensitive data.

5. Types of cloud computing services

This is the answer to “the three types”

The three types of cloud computing services are SaaS, PaaS and IaaS. They are not AWS, Azure and Alibaba Cloud — those are providers, which is a different question entirely. Tutorial 4 answers this wrongly; the answer below is the correct one.

SaaS, PaaS and IaaS compared
ServiceWhat the client getsExamples
SaaS
Software as a Service		 The ability to use the provider's software application over the internet by subscription, from anywhere via the web. The client manages nothing. Google applications, Salesforce
PaaS
Platform as a Service		 A platform on which the client deploys and hosts their own applications, without having to worry about infrastructure, storage, servers, network or hardware. Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure
IaaS
Infrastructure as a Service		 Just the hardware and the network. The client installs and develops the software and applications themselves. Amazon AWS, IBM

Read the table as a ladder of how much the client still has to do: with SaaS, nothing but use it; with PaaS, build and deploy the application; with IaaS, install everything on top of bare hardware.

6. Advantages of cloud computing

  • Cost efficiency
  • Backup and recovery
  • Automated software updates — the server regularly updates software, including security patches, with no user involvement in maintenance
  • 24/7 availability
  • Security — your data is not lost
  • Quick deployment
  • Easy access to information — from any device with an internet connection

Two further advantages appear in the tutorial: almost unlimited storage, and flexibility or scalability — companies can increase or decrease their resources as their needs change. Cloud computing also contributes to environmental sustainability, by reducing IT expenses and resource footprint through shared physical hardware.

7. Disadvantages of cloud computing

Technical issues
The service can go down, and you cannot fix it yourself.
Security in the cloud
You are surrendering your company's sensitive information to a third-party provider, which can increase security risk.
Inflexibility
Choosing a cloud provider locks you into their proprietary applications and formats, making it hard to move later.
Prone to attack
Storing information in the cloud leaves the client vulnerable to external hacking attacks.
Legislation and regulation
Legal and regulatory constraints on where and how data may be stored.

The tutorial adds possible downtime to this list.

8. Internet of Things

IoT — Internet of Things

Extending the power of the internet beyond computers and smartphones to a whole range of other things, processes and environments. It is the idea of connecting everyday devices to the internet so that they can collect and send data over a wireless network without needing a person to do it manually.

How it works — three main parts

  1. Sensors — these act as a “digital nervous system”, collecting data from the real world: GPS for location, cameras and microphones for sight and sound, and sensors for temperature, motion and so on.
  2. Connectivity — the collected data is turned into digital form and sent over a network (Wi-Fi, cellular and so on).
  3. People and processes — the data is used in systems where information flows both ways, combining data, people and processes to make better decisions.

Example: a smart refrigerator senses that the milk is low, sends that data over the network, and automatically adds milk to your shopping list — all without you doing anything.

One of the standing concerns with IoT devices is the need for robust security protocols to protect against unauthorised data access.

9. Web analytics

Web analytics

The collection, reporting and analysis of website data. It matters because it shows what users are actually doing on your site.

The three types of web analytics data
Type of dataWhat it tells you
Audience dataWho is coming — for example the number of visits.
Audience behaviourWhat they do — for example the most frequently visited pages.
Campaign dataWhere they came from — which campaigns drove the most traffic.

The standard tool is Google Analytics.

10. Descriptive, predictive and prescriptive analytics

Descriptive analytics
Looks at historical data to understand past performance. What happened?
Predictive analytics
Uses historical data, statistical models and machine learning to forecast future trends. What is likely to happen?
Prescriptive analytics
Goes a step further, using algorithms and AI to suggest specific actions to take advantage of those predictions. What should we do about it?

11. Blockchain

Blockchain

A system of recording information in a way that makes it difficult or impossible to change, hack or cheat the system. It is a type of database.

Blockchain compared with a conventional database
BlockchainConventional database
Data structure Collects information together in groups known as blocks, each holding a set of information. Structures its data into tables.
Consequence Records are chained and effectively immutable. Records can be edited by whoever holds the rights.

The examinable difference is the way the data is structured: blocks versus tables.

12. Artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI)

Intelligent systems with the ability to think and learn. AI can augment human work, and includes natural language processing, machine learning and machine vision.

Neural networks

Machine learning and artificial intelligence models that simulate the way biological neural networks in the human brain work, in order to model complex problems.

13. Big data

Big data

The term for a collection of data sets so large and complex that it becomes difficult to process using on-hand database management tools or traditional data processing applications.

Why it is hard to process

The challenges are in capture, curation, storage, search, sharing, transfer, analysis and visualisation. Traditional tools were built for data that fits on one machine and arrives at a manageable rate; big data breaks all of those assumptions at once, so it fails on scale rather than on any single operation.

How big data differs from small data

The difference follows directly from the definition. Small data is small and simple enough to be handled by ordinary, on-hand database management tools and traditional data processing applications — it fits on one machine, one person can read and understand it, and a conventional table structure holds it comfortably. Big data is defined by exceeding exactly those limits:

Big data compared with small data
Small dataBig data
SizeManageable volumeSo large it becomes difficult to handle
ComplexitySimple, usually structuredComplex, and varied in form
Tools neededOn-hand database management tools are enoughTraditional tools cannot cope; specialised processing is required
Main difficultyOrdinary querying and reportingCapture, curation, storage, search, sharing, transfer, analysis and visualisation
