1. The three main concerns

When it comes to computer security, the three most significant concerns are privacy, security and ethics. This is a favourite exam question, and it is also the one people most often answer wrongly — see the warning under it.

Privacy
Threats to personal privacy, and the ways of protecting against them.
Security
Access to sensitive information, and the control and securing of hardware and software.
Ethics
The way the actions of individuals and companies affect society.
Do not confuse these two lists

The three main concerns are Privacy, Security and Ethics. The three computer security issues are Intrusion, Sabotage and Natural disasters (section 8). They are separate lists answering separate questions, and swapping them is a very easy way to lose marks. If the question says concerns, answer Privacy / Security / Ethics.

2. Privacy and the three privacy issues

Privacy

An individual's ability to eliminate the collection, use and sale of confidential personal information about them.

There are three primary privacy issues:

Accuracy
The responsibility of those who collect data to make sure it is correct.
Property
Who owns the data, and who holds the rights to how software is used.
Access
The control over who may reach the data — restricting it to authorised users.

3. How personal data is gathered

Personal data is collected in several different ways:

  • Large organisations — the records companies and institutions hold on you
  • Use of cookies — small files left in your browser by sites you visit
  • Information resellers and brokers — companies whose business is buying and selling personal data
  • Private networks — the network you sit on can see what you do
  • The internet and the web — general browsing activity
  • Online identity — whatever you post about yourself
  • Web bugs and spyware — hidden trackers and installed monitoring software

Employee monitoring software

Many businesses search employees' email and computer files using employee monitoring software — for example Flexi­Server, InterGuard or Deputy. The monitoring typically covers:

  • Application usage — which programs are opened and for how long
  • Internet activity and social media — sites visited during work hours
  • Work-related activity — general productivity and output

4. Cookies

Cookie

A small data or text file deposited on your hard disk by websites you have visited. Cookies are harmless in themselves, but because they store information about your browsing habits and preferences, users are right to worry that a third party could make use of them.

First-party and third-party cookies compared
TypeWho generates it
First-party cookie Generated by the site you are currently visiting.
Third-party cookie Generated by an advertising company working with the site you are visiting.

5. What your computer keeps about you

Critical information about your activity is stored on the hard drive in these places:

History files
Include the locations or addresses of the sites you have visited.
Temporary internet files
Saved files from websites you have visited (the browser cache).
Cookies
Text files stored on your computer on behalf of the sites you visit.
Search engines
Services such as Google can keep a record of your search history.
Browsers
Keep track of all of your activity.

6. Privacy threats

Computer monitoring software
The most dangerous category — software such as SpyAgent tracks email, chats and other activity.
Keystroke loggers
Record activity and every keystroke typed, and can hijack the browser.
Spyware
Records and reports your internet activity, and changes the browser to manipulate what you see.
Web bugs
Invisible images or HTML code hidden inside an email message or a web page.
Online identity
The information people post about themselves online — a threat you create yourself.

7. Protecting your privacy online

  1. Use privacy modes when browsing.
  2. Install anti-spyware programs.
  3. Use free web-based services rather than handing over more than you need to.
  4. Fill in information only when you actually need to.
  5. Do not save account numbers or passwords in the browser.
  6. Turn off cookies.

8. Computer security issues

These are the three issues — not the three concerns from section 1.

Intrusion
Hackers gaining access to a system without permission.
Sabotage
Using technology — viruses, zombies and the like — to crack or damage the network.
Natural disasters
Acts of nature such as flood or fire.
From Tutorial 3 Part 2

The tutorial also asks for the two possible security disasters that organisations worry about: physical disasters (natural events such as floods and earthquakes) and cyber attacks (which are what lead to unauthorised data access). This is a third, separate grouping — keep it apart from the two lists above.

9. Cybercrime

Cybercrime

A criminal offence that involves a computer and a network or the internet.

Types of computer crime and their descriptions
Computer crimeDescription
Identity theftIllegal assumption of a person's identity for financial or other gain.
Internet scamsScams carried out over the internet.
RansomwareMalicious software that encrypts your data and ransoms the password back to you.
Denial of service (DoS)An attempt to slow down or stop a computer by flooding it with requests.
Distributed denial of service (DDoS)Similar to DoS, but using several computers to make the repeated requests.
Data manipulationUnauthorised access to a computer network in order to alter data.
Cyber­bullyingUsing the internet or any device to hurt or embarrass another person.
Sexual predatorA person seeking, or attempting to obtain, sexual contact with another person online.

10. Types of hackers

The three hacker types
TypeMotive
White hat (ethical)Break into systems for non-malicious purposes, usually to find and report weaknesses.
Black hatBreak into systems in order to destroy or steal.
Grey hatBreak in illegally, but to flaunt their expertise or to try to sell their services afterwards.

11. Social engineering

Social engineering

The practice of manipulating people into divulging private data, through acts such as identity theft, internet scams and data manipulation. The target is the person, not the machine.

The four main types

Phishing
A technique used by scammers to trick internet users with a fake but official-looking site or message — typically an email that appears to come from a reputable source and asks you to reveal personal information.
Pretexting
The attacker focuses on creating a good pretext — a fabricated scenario — and uses it to manipulate the victim into handing over information.
Baiting
Similar to phishing, but the lure is a free gift or physical item rather than a message. The classic example is an infected USB drive left in a car park for someone to pick up.
Quid Pro Quo
Similar to baiting, but the attacker offers a service instead of a good — for example someone posing as IT support offering to “fix” your machine in exchange for your password.
Spelling

The term is Quid Pro Quo (Latin, “something for something”). If you have seen it written “Quad Pro Qua” anywhere, that is a typo — including in the original tutorial sheet.

There are further types worth knowing the concept of: vishing (voice phishing, carried out over a phone call), smishing (phishing by SMS) and scareware.

Scareware

Software that pretends to be security software in order to frighten users into buying it — typically a pop-up claiming your machine is infected and urging you to download a removal tool, which then asks for payment details.

How to tell them apart in a scenario question

12. Malware

Malware

Software designed by crackers or computer criminals to damage a computer system.

The three most common malicious programs:

Virus, worm and Trojan horse compared
ProgramWhat it doesKey distinction
Virus Migrates through networks and attaches itself to different programs and files. Requires a host program to spread.
Worm Fills the computer or server with self-replicating information. Replicates autonomously, with no host program and no user action.
Trojan horse A program disguised as something else, or one that triggers a hidden action. Embedded in a legitimate program; relies on the user running it.

All three are malicious programs that spread and cause damage — that is the “how are they similar” half of the question. The difference is purely in how they get there: a virus needs a host, a worm needs nothing, and a Trojan needs you to be fooled into running it.

13. Malicious hardware

Criminals also use hardware to commit crimes. The most common ways are:

Zombies
A computer connected to a network that has been compromised by a hacker or a virus, and is then controlled remotely.
Rogue Wi-Fi hotspots
Networks imitating legitimate free Wi-Fi in order to capture the data passing through them.
Virus-infected USB flash drives
Left lying around on purpose, in the hope that somebody will pick one up and plug it in.

14. Measures to protect computer security

  1. Restrict access. Use security guards, and require proper authentication.
  2. Use firewalls. Special hardware and software acting as a security buffer between a private network and all external networks.
  3. Install security suites. A collection of programs designed to protect your privacy and security while you are online.
  4. Stay away from dodgy websites. Sites served over plain http:// and badly built commercial sites may record your activity.
  5. Be alert to email scams. Use an anti-spam program to filter incoming mail.
  6. Back up your data and system so that a disaster does not become a catastrophe.

15. Encryption

Encryption

Coding information so that it is unreadable to everyone except those who are permitted to read it and hold the key to decode the message.

Encrypt

To change plain text into encrypted text (and, with the key, back again).

Cases where encryption is used:

  • Email
  • Files
  • Websites
  • VPNs, to create an encrypted connection
  • Wireless network encryption, using WPA or WPA2

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages and disadvantages of data encryption
AdvantagesDisadvantages
People without permission cannot read or spy on your information. Reduces the speed of data flow and consumes bandwidth while data is being encrypted.
Reduces hacking incidents and the damage done when data is intercepted. Makes file sizes larger.
Protects data in transit, at rest, and across untrusted networks. Adds complexity to the communication, and to key management.

16. Ethics

Ethics
A standard of moral conduct.
Computer ethics
Guidelines for the morally acceptable use of computers.
Copyright and digital rights
Give content creators the right to control the use and distribution of their work.
Plagiarism
Using someone else's work or ideas without giving credit to the original author.
