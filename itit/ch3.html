Electronic Mail

1. What email is

Email

A form of communication in which electronic messages are created and transferred between two or more devices connected to a network.

2. Email compared with postal mail

Similarities

  1. You put a message into an electronic envelope and place an electronic address on it.
  2. Your message may come back to you if it cannot be delivered.
  3. The sender has to know your address before anything can be sent.
  4. You can keep in touch with friends through it.
  5. Your mail may be delivered to, and read by, someone else.
  6. Not every piece of mail you receive will be pleasant and friendly.

Differences

  • Email is so easy to handle that it has become an extremely useful tool for reaching people.
  • When there is a problem, your email may go to a computer postmaster rather than a human one.
  • Because email is very easy to create, it can lead to sending responses that you later regret.

3. Advantages of email over standard mail delivery

  • Ease of handling. Email is simple to write, send and file, whereas standard mail involves many physical steps before the message reaches the receiver.
  • Speed. An email reaches its destination in seconds; standard mail can take days.
  • Reach and cost. The same message can go to many recipients at once, at no extra cost per copy.

4. How sending an email works

Seven steps, from clicking send to the message being read:

  1. The sender composes an email in their email client and clicks send.
  2. The email client contacts the SMTP server of the sender's email service provider.
  3. The SMTP server checks whether the recipient's domain is the same as the sender's. If it is, the email is redirected internally.
  4. If the recipient's domain is different, the SMTP server queries a DNS server to find the IP address of the recipient's mail server.
  5. The email is then sent to the recipient's SMTP server over the internet.
  6. On arrival, the recipient's SMTP server passes the email to a mail delivery system, which places it in the recipient's mailbox.
  7. The recipient's email client retrieves the message from the mailbox using either POP3 or IMAP, so that it can be read.
Where each protocol appears

SMTP does steps 2–6 — it is the sending protocol, and it is used on both ends. DNS appears only at step 4. POP3 or IMAP appear only at step 7, on the receiving side. If a question asks “which protocol retrieves mail”, the answer is never SMTP.

5. The protocols

Protocol
The rules that determine how the internet will handle the message packets flowing over it.
SMTP — Simple Mail Transfer Protocol
Decides which paths an email message will take on the internet when you send it. It is the outgoing protocol.
POP — Post Office Protocol
Handles incoming messages, downloading them to the local device. Little used nowadays.
IMAP — Internet Message Access Protocol
A protocol for retrieving mail messages from a server. Like POP, but it lets the client read the mail on the server without downloading it to the local device — which is why the same mailbox stays in sync across your phone and laptop.
MIME — Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions
A protocol that specifies how to encode non-text data, such as graphics and sounds, so that it can travel over the internet.

6. Mail server and mail client

Mail server
A hardware and software system that determines, from the recipient's address, which of several possible electronic routes to send the message on.
Mail client software
Software that requests mail delivery from the mail server to your internet device.

7. The email address

Email address

Uniquely identifies an individual or organisation connected to the internet. It has two parts, separated by an at sign (@).

The two parts of an email address
PartWhat it identifies
UsernameIdentifies a person within an organisation.
Domain nameSpecifies the server to which the email is to be delivered.

8. Email message components

To
The field where the email address of the intended recipient is entered.
Subject
Essentially a title, or a very short summary of the email.
CC — Carbon Copy
Sends someone a copy of the email even though they are not its intended recipient. Everyone can see who was copied.
BCC — Blind Carbon Copy
Anyone on the BCC list receives a copy of the message, but their name is not included in the message headers, and nobody else who received the message knows they were sent a copy.

The difference between CC and BCC is entirely one of visibility: both deliver a copy, but a CC recipient is visible to everyone and a BCC recipient is hidden from everyone. BCC is the privacy option.

9. Email etiquette

  1. Include a clear subject line, and do not shout.
  2. Always use an appropriate greeting.
  3. Only use shorthand if you know the recipient well.
  4. Think before you smile — emoticons are not always appropriate.
  5. Be wary of using humour; it travels badly in writing.
  6. Always spell check.

Why it matters

Email is the medium professional life runs on — you cannot deal with colleagues, teammates or other organisations without it. Most people take email seriously and judge you on it, so good etiquette protects your professional reputation, prevents misunderstandings, and saves everyone the unnecessary back-and-forth that a badly written message causes.

10. Email netiquette

  • Avoid writing in all capital letters — it reads as shouting.
  • Keep your message simple and short.
  • Include a descriptive subject line and a signature.
  • Do not assume everyone likes to receive jokes.
  • Use a spell checker.
